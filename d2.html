2.1.1 · The five words

Distance, displacement, speed, velocity, acceleration

QuantityDefinitionTypeUnit
DistanceTotal length of path travelledscalarm
DisplacementDistance in a stated direction from a fixed point (straight-line distance)vectorm
SpeedRate of change of distance (distance ÷ time)scalarm s⁻¹
VelocityRate of change of displacementvectorm s⁻¹
AccelerationRate of change of velocityvectorm s⁻²

Worked example

A runner does one full lap of a 400 m track in 50 s. Distance = 400 m, so average speed = 8.0 m s⁻¹. She ends where she started, so displacement = 0 and average velocity = 0.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

You walk all around the playground and come back to the door. Your legs walked a long way (distance). But you are right back at the door, so you "moved" zero from where you started (displacement).

Trap

"Acceleration is the rate of change of speed" loses the mark. It is rate of change of velocity. A car going round a bend at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.

2.1.2 and 2.1.4 · Displacement–time graphs

Gradient of an s–t graph is velocity

v = Δs / Δt  =  gradient of s–t graph
  • For a curve, draw a tangent at the point and find its gradient. Use a large triangle (more than half the length of the tangent).
  • A negative gradient means moving back towards the start (negative velocity).
  • On a distance–time graph the line can never go down; on a displacement–time graph it can.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

The steeper the line, the faster you're going. A flat line means you stopped to tie your shoes.

2.1.3 and 2.1.5 · Velocity–time graphs

Gradient is acceleration, area is displacement

Worked example (from the graph)

0–4 s: a = (12 − 0)/4 = +3.0 m s⁻² 4–10 s: a = 0 (constant velocity) 10–13 s: a = (0 − 12)/3 = −4.0 m s⁻² area = ½×4×12 + 6×12 + ½×3×12 = 114 m

Trap: area below the axis

Area under the time axis is negative displacement. For displacement, subtract it. For total distance, add its size. Also check the axes: if the graph is speed–time, there is no negative area.

2.1.6 · Derivation

Four equations from two definitions

Only valid for constant (uniform) acceleration in a straight line. Symbols: s displacement, u initial velocity, v final velocity, a acceleration, t time.

  1. From the definition of acceleration, a = (v − u)/t, so v = u + at … (1)
  2. From the definition of velocity, s = average velocity × t. With uniform acceleration the v–t graph is a straight line, so average velocity = (u + v)/2. So s = ½(u + v)t … (2)
  3. Substitute (1) into (2): s = ½(u + u + at)t, so s = ut + ½at² … (3)
  4. From (1), t = (v − u)/a. Put into (2): s = (u + v)(v − u)/(2a) = (v² − u²)/(2a), so v² = u² + 2as … (4)

Graph view of equation (3)

Area under the v–t graph = rectangle (u × t) + triangle (½ × t × at) = ut + ½at².

Data sheet: s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as are printed on page 2 of Papers 1 and 2. The other two you must recall.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

If you speed up steadily from 2 to 6 on a scooter, your "middle speed" is 4. Multiply your middle speed by the time and you get how far you went.

2.1.7 · Problem solving

A method that works every time

  1. Choose a positive direction and write it down.
  2. List s, u, v, a, t. Fill in three known values with signs. Mark the one you want.
  3. Pick the equation that contains the three knowns and the unknown, and leaves out the fifth.
  4. Solve, then check the sign and size make sense.

Worked example: braking car

A car at 25 m s⁻¹ brakes uniformly and stops in 50 m. Find the deceleration and the time taken.

+ = direction of travel u = 25, v = 0, s = 50, a = ?, t = ? v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 625 + 100a → a = −6.25 m s⁻² v = u + at → 0 = 25 − 6.25t → t = 4.0 s check: s = ½(u+v)t = ½(25)(4.0) = 50 m ✓

If the driver's reaction time is 0.70 s, the car travels 25 × 0.70 = 17.5 m at constant speed first. Total stopping distance = 67.5 m.

Trap

Using SUVAT across a change in acceleration. Split the motion at each change (e.g. reaction time, then braking) and solve each stage separately.

2.1.7 · Free fall

Free fall: a = g = 9.81 m s⁻² downwards

With no air resistance, every object near Earth's surface accelerates downwards at g = 9.81 m s⁻² (given on the data sheet), whatever its mass, and whether it is going up, down, or is momentarily at rest.

Worked example: stone thrown up from a cliff

A stone is thrown vertically up at 15 m s⁻¹ from the edge of a cliff 20 m high. Find (a) the maximum height above the cliff, (b) the time to hit the ground below, (c) its impact speed.

+ = upwards, a = −9.81 (a) top: v = 0. v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 225 − 19.62s → s = 11.5 m (b) s = −20 (below start). s = ut + ½at² −20 = 15t − 4.905t² → 4.905t² − 15t − 20 = 0 t = [15 + √(225 + 392.4)] / 9.81 = 4.06 s (c) v² = 15² + 2(−9.81)(−20) = 617.4 → v = −24.8 m s⁻¹ impact speed 24.8 m s⁻¹ downwards

Trap: a = 0 at the top

At the highest point v = 0 but a = −9.81 m s⁻². Gravity never switches off.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Throw a ball up. Gravity keeps pulling it down the whole time. Going up, it slows down. At the top it stops for a blink. Then it speeds up on the way down.

Graph favourite in Paper 1

The bouncing ball v–t graph

A ball is dropped from rest and bounces several times. Take upwards as positive and ignore air resistance.

  • Straight lines: acceleration is constant (g) in flight.
  • Same gradient going up and coming down.
  • Near-vertical jump at each bounce: huge upward acceleration during the short contact time.
  • Rebound speed smaller each time: kinetic energy lost on impact.
  • Crossing the axis = top of the bounce.

2.1.8 · Experiment

Determining g with a falling object

Apparatus

  • Steel ball held by an electromagnet
  • Trap-door (or light gate) below, connected to an electronic timer
  • Metre rule to measure height h

Method

  1. Switching off the magnet releases the ball from rest and starts the timer.
  2. The ball opens the trap-door and stops the timer: time t.
  3. Measure h from the bottom of the ball to the trap-door.
  4. Repeat each height and average t. Use at least 5 different heights.

Analysis

u = 0, so s = ut + ½at² becomes h = ½gt². Plot h against t². It is a straight line through the origin with gradient = g/2, so g = 2 × gradient.

Alternative: two light gates measure speeds v₁ and v₂ a distance h apart. Then v₂² = v₁² + 2gh.

Improving it

Use a small dense ball (air resistance negligible). Use a graph rather than one reading. A delay in releasing the magnet is a systematic error.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Drop a ball from different heights and time how long it takes to land. Higher drops take longer, in a special pattern. That pattern tells us how strong gravity's pull is.

2.1.9 · Projectiles

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent

Horizontal

No force (no air resistance), so a = 0. Velocity stays constant. Use s = vt.

Vertical

Weight acts, so a = g downwards. Use SUVAT. Time links the two.

Worked example

A ball leaves a 45.0 m cliff horizontally at 20.0 m s⁻¹. Find the time of flight, range, and impact velocity.

vertical (+ down): u = 0, s = 45.0, a = 9.81 45.0 = ½(9.81)t² → t = 3.03 s horizontal: range = 20.0 × 3.03 = 60.6 m vertical speed at impact = 9.81 × 3.03 = 29.7 m s⁻¹ speed = √(20.0² + 29.7²) = 35.8 m s⁻¹ angle below horizontal = tan⁻¹(29.7/20.0) = 56°
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Roll a ball off a table and drop another at the same moment. They hit the floor together. Rolling sideways doesn't make the ball fall any slower.

2.1.9 · Projectiles at an angle

Resolve first, then treat each direction separately

Worked example

A ball is kicked at 25 m s⁻¹ at 40° above level ground. Find the time of flight, range and maximum height.

uₓ = 25 cos 40° = 19.15 m s⁻¹ (constant) u_y = 25 sin 40° = 16.07 m s⁻¹ (+ up, a = −9.81) top: v_y = 0 → t_up = 16.07/9.81 = 1.638 s time of flight = 2 × 1.638 = 3.28 s (level ground) range = 19.15 × 3.276 = 62.7 m max height: 0 = 16.07² − 2(9.81)h → h = 13.2 m
At the top of the pathValue
Vertical velocity0
Horizontal velocityunchanged: uₓ (not zero)
Speedminimum, equal to uₓ
Acceleration9.81 m s⁻² downwards

With air resistance (covered qualitatively in Dynamics 3.2), range and height are smaller and the path is no longer symmetrical: the way down is steeper.

Exam traps · Topic 2

Where marks are lost

Patterns from 9702 examiner reports and mark schemes for kinematics. The Past papers slide shows the exact questions.

Speed instead of velocity

Defining acceleration as rate of change of speed, or velocity as distance per time.

Velocity: rate of change of displacement. Acceleration: rate of change of velocity.

Sign errors

Mixing up directions, especially with g and with objects thrown upwards.

State "+ = up" before you start. Then g = −9.81.

Missing the ½

Writing s = ut + at².

It is on the data sheet. Copy it from there.

Distance vs displacement from area

Adding areas below the axis when displacement is asked for (or subtracting when distance is asked).

Read the question word: distance or displacement.

Horizontal velocity at the top

Saying a projectile's velocity is zero at the top.

Only the vertical component is zero.

Mass in free fall

Thinking a heavier ball falls faster (no air resistance).

a = g for every mass.

Gradient from a tiny triangle

Reading a tangent's gradient with points close together.

Use points more than half the line length apart.

Doubling time of flight wrongly

Using 2 × t_up when the landing point is lower than the launch point (e.g. off a cliff).

Only double on level ground. Otherwise solve s = ut + ½at².
