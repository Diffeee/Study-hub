4.1.1 · Centre of gravity

All the weight, acting at one point

Centre of gravityThe point at which the whole weight of an object may be considered to act.
  • For a uniform object (a uniform beam, a uniform rod) it is at the geometric centre. "Uniform" in a question is telling you this.
  • In moments problems, draw the weight as one arrow from the centre of gravity.
  • If a body hangs freely from a pivot, its centre of gravity settles directly below the pivot, so the weight has no moment about it.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Balance a ruler on one finger. There is one special spot where it doesn't tip either way. That spot is where all its heaviness seems to be squashed into one point.

4.1.2 · Moments

Force × perpendicular distance

Moment of a forceThe product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.
moment = F × d⊥   (N m)

Worked example

d⊥ = 0.25 sin 60° = 0.217 m. Moment = 40 × 0.217 = 8.7 N m. Equivalent method: component of force perpendicular to the handle = 40 sin 60° = 34.6 N, times 0.25 m = 8.7 N m.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Pushing a door near the hinges is hard. Pushing far from the hinges is easy. The further from the turning point you push, the bigger the turning effect.

4.1.3 and 4.1.4 · Couples

A couple turns but does not move

CoupleA pair of equal and opposite (parallel) forces whose lines of action do not coincide. It produces rotation only: the resultant force is zero, so there is no translation.
Torque of a coupleThe product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between the lines of action of the forces.

Worked example

Torque = 15 × 0.40 = 6.0 N m. Check by moments about the centre: 15 × 0.20 + 15 × 0.20 = 6.0 N m. The torque of a couple is the same about any point.

Trap

Multiplying by both forces (2F × d), or using the radius instead of the distance between the lines of action.

4.2.1 and 4.2.2 · Principle of moments

Two conditions for equilibrium

Principle of momentsFor a body in (rotational) equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that same point.

Condition 1

No resultant force: forces up = forces down, forces left = forces right.

Condition 2

No resultant torque (moment) about any point.

Worked example

moments about A (removes R_A): R_B × 6.0 = 300 × 3.0 + 900 × 2.0 = 2700 → R_B = 450 N forces: R_A + R_B = 300 + 900 → R_A = 750 N

Tip: take moments about the point where an unknown force acts. Its moment is zero, so it vanishes from the equation.

4.2.3 · Three forces in equilibrium

The forces close into a triangle

If three coplanar forces keep a body in equilibrium, drawing them tip to tail makes a closed triangle: the resultant is zero.

Worked example

A 50 N lamp hangs from a cable at 30° to the vertical, pulled sideways by a horizontal rope. Find the tension T and the horizontal pull H.

vertical: T cos 30° = 50 → T = 57.7 N horizontal: H = T sin 30° → H = 28.9 N (or H = 50 tan 30°)
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Three friends pull a toy with ropes and it doesn't move. If you draw each pull as an arrow, end to start, the arrows join up into a triangle and you end where you began. That means nobody is winning.

4.3.1 · Density

Mass per unit volume

DensityMass per unit volume. ρ = m / V, unit kg m⁻³.

Worked example

A block measures 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm × 2.0 cm and has mass 108 g. Find its density in SI units.

V = 5.0 × 4.0 × 2.0 = 40 cm³ = 40 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ m³ ρ = 0.108 / 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 2700 kg m⁻³ (aluminium)
MaterialTypical density / kg m⁻³
Air (room conditions)about 1.2
Water1000
Aluminiumabout 2700
Steel / ironabout 7800–7900
Explain it to a 5-year-old

A pillow and a brick of the same size: the brick is much heavier because its "stuff" is packed in tighter. That tightness is density.

4.3.2 · Pressure

Normal force per unit area

PressureForce acting normally (perpendicular) per unit area. p = F / A, unit Pa = N m⁻².

Worked example

A person of weight 600 N stands on both feet, total contact area 0.040 m². p = 600 / 0.040 = 1.5 × 10⁴ Pa. On one stiletto heel of area 1.0 cm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², the same weight gives p = 6.0 × 10⁶ Pa, 400 times more.

  • Pressure is a scalar: at a point in a fluid it acts equally in all directions.
  • If a force is at an angle to a surface, only its normal component counts.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Pressing a pin into a board is easy because all your push goes into a tiny point. Pressing with your flat thumb doesn't go in, even with the same push, because it's spread out.

4.3.3 and 4.3.4 · Derivation and use

Deriving Δp = ρgΔh

Consider a column of liquid of density ρ, height Δh and cross-sectional area A.

  1. Volume of column: V = A Δh.
  2. Mass (from density): m = ρV = ρAΔh.
  3. Weight: W = mg = ρAΔh g.
  4. Pressure (from definition) = W / A = ρAΔhg / A, so Δp = ρgΔh.

A cancels, so the pressure depends only on depth, not on the width or shape of the container.

Worked example

A diver is 12 m below the surface of water (ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³). Atmospheric pressure is 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa.

extra pressure from water: Δp = 1000 × 9.81 × 12 = 1.18 × 10⁵ Pa total pressure on diver = 1.01 × 10⁵ + 1.18 × 10⁵ = 2.19 × 10⁵ Pa

Trap

Δp = ρgΔh is the pressure due to the liquid. If the question asks for the total pressure, add atmospheric pressure. Read which one is asked.

4.3.5 and 4.3.6 · Upthrust

Upthrust comes from a pressure difference

upthrust = p₂A − p₁A = (ρ_f g h) × A = ρ_f g V
F = ρ g V   (ρ = density of the FLUID, V = volume submerged)

Archimedes' principle: the upthrust equals the weight of fluid displaced. (Sideways forces on the vertical faces cancel.)

Worked example

An aluminium block (ρ = 2700 kg m⁻³) of volume 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ is fully submerged in water.

upthrust = 1000 × 9.81 × 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 9.81 N weight = 2700 × 1.0 × 10⁻³ × 9.81 = 26.5 N tension needed to hold it still = 26.5 − 9.81 = 16.7 N

4.3.6 · Applying upthrust

A floating object: upthrust = weight

A floating object is in equilibrium, so upthrust = weight. Only part of it is under the surface.

ρ_fluid × g × V_submerged = ρ_object × g × V_total fraction submerged = V_submerged / V_total = ρ_object / ρ_fluid

Worked example

Wood of density 600 kg m⁻³ in water: fraction submerged = 600/1000 = 0.60. Ice (about 920 kg m⁻³) in fresh water: about 92% is under the surface, which is why most of an iceberg is hidden.

  • Object denser than fluid: weight > maximum upthrust, so it sinks.
  • Object less dense: it rises until it floats with upthrust = weight.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Push a beach ball under water and it shoves back up hard. Water pushes up more from underneath than it pushes down from above, because deeper water squeezes harder.

Exam traps · Topic 4

Where marks are lost

Patterns from 9702 examiner reports and mark schemes for this topic. The Past papers slide shows the exact questions.

Moment definition missing "perpendicular"

"Force × distance" scores nothing.

Force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

Using the wrong distance

Measuring along the beam to the point of application when the force is at an angle.

Find d⊥ with trig, or use the perpendicular component of the force.

Principle of moments without conditions

Missing "in equilibrium" or "about the same point".

Include both phrases.

Equilibrium: only one condition

Stating only "resultant force is zero".

Zero resultant force AND zero resultant torque.

Couple torque doubled

Using 2F × d.

One force × perpendicular distance between the lines of action.

Wrong density in upthrust

Using the object's density in F = ρgV.

ρ is the fluid's density. V is the volume submerged.

Explaining upthrust

"Water pushes up" or "it is less dense" without the mechanism.

Pressure increases with depth, so the force on the bottom surface is greater than on the top.

Unit conversions

cm² and cm³ treated like cm.

1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m², 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³.
