3.1.1 and 3.1.6 · Mass and weight

Mass resists change. Weight is a force.

Mass (syllabus wording)The property of an object that resists change in motion. Scalar, unit kg. The same everywhere.
WeightThe effect of a gravitational field on a mass: W = mg. A force, so a vector, unit N. Depends on where you are.
W = m g    (g = 9.81 m s⁻² = 9.81 N kg⁻¹ near Earth)

Worked example

An astronaut of mass 80 kg. On Earth: W = 80 × 9.81 = 785 N. On the Moon (g = 1.62 N kg⁻¹): W = 130 N. Her mass is still 80 kg, so she is just as hard to push sideways or stop.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Mass is how much "stuff" you are made of, and how hard you are to get moving or to stop. Weight is how hard the Earth pulls that stuff down. On the Moon the pull is weaker, but you are still made of the same amount of stuff.

3.1.5 · Newton's laws of motion

Three laws, learned word for word

First lawA body continues at rest or at constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant (external) force.
Second lawThe resultant force on a body is proportional to its rate of change of momentum, and acts in the direction of the change in momentum. With SI units the constant is 1, so F = Δp/Δt.
Third lawIf body A exerts a force on body B, then body B exerts a force on body A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

1st: a toy car on a smooth floor keeps rolling until something stops it.

2nd: push harder and it speeds up faster. A heavy truck needs a bigger push.

3rd: when you push a wall, the wall pushes you back just as hard.

Trap: "constant speed" in the first law

Write constant velocity. Constant speed round a bend still needs a resultant force. Also write resultant force, not just "a force".

3.1.2 · Resultant force

F = ma, and a points the same way as F

resultant force F = m a

F = ma comes from the second law when mass is constant: F = Δ(mv)/Δt = m Δv/Δt = ma. The acceleration is always in the direction of the resultant force, which is not always the direction of motion (a braking car moves forward but accelerates backward).

  1. Draw a free-body diagram: only forces on the one object.
  2. Choose a positive direction (usually the direction of acceleration).
  3. Resultant force = (forces in + direction) − (forces in − direction) = ma.

Worked example: person in a lift

A 60 kg person stands in a lift accelerating upwards at 1.5 m s⁻². Find the normal (contact) force R from the floor.

+ = up R − mg = ma R = m(g + a) = 60 × (9.81 + 1.5) = 679 N

If the lift instead slows down while going up (a = −1.5 m s⁻²): R = 60 × 8.31 = 499 N. A scale reads R, so you "feel" heavier or lighter, but your weight mg = 589 N has not changed.

3.1.2 · Applying F = ma

Two bodies joined together

Treat the system as a whole to get the acceleration, then treat one body alone to get the force between them.

Worked example

whole system: 2000 − 200 − 200 = (1200 + 400)a → a = 1.0 m s⁻² trailer alone: T − 200 = 400 × 1.0 → T = 600 N

Check with the car alone: 2000 − 200 − T = 1200 × 1.0 gives T = 600 N. ✓

Trap

Using the driving force (2000 N) or the total mass when working on just one body. Each equation uses only the forces on, and the mass of, the body you chose.

3.1.5 · Newton's third law

Weight and normal force are not a third-law pair

A third-law pair always: acts on two different bodies, is the same type of force, is equal in size, opposite in direction, and acts along the same line.

Book resting on a table: forceIts third-law partner
Earth pulls book down (weight, gravitational)Book pulls Earth up (gravitational)
Table pushes book up (normal, contact)Book pushes table down (contact)

Trap

Weight and normal reaction on the book are equal and opposite only because the book is in equilibrium (first law). They act on the same body and are different types, so they are not a third-law pair. In an accelerating lift they are not even equal.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Forces always come as a pair of twins, one on each of two things. If you push your friend on skates, your friend pushes you back too, so you both roll apart.

3.1.3 and 3.1.4 · Momentum and force

Force is rate of change of momentum

p = m v   (kg m s⁻¹ = N s)
F = Δp / Δt

Momentum is a vector: its direction is the direction of the velocity. Rearranged, F Δt = Δp, so the area under a force–time graph equals the change in momentum.

Worked example 1: ball hits a wall

A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at 20 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 15 m s⁻¹. Contact time 0.050 s. Find the average force.

+ = away from wall: u = −20, v = +15 Δp = 0.15 × (15 − (−20)) = 0.15 × 35 = 5.25 N s F = 5.25 / 0.050 = 105 N (away from the wall)

Worked example 2: water jet (mass changing)

Water hits a wall at 8.0 m s⁻¹ at 2.0 kg per second and stops. Momentum lost per second = 2.0 × 8.0 = 16 kg m s⁻², so force = 16 N. Here F = ma cannot be used directly, but F = Δp/Δt can.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Momentum is "how hard something is to stop". A slow truck and a fast cricket ball can both be hard to stop. Stopping it quickly needs a big force; stopping it gently over a longer time needs a smaller force. That's why airbags help.

3.2.1 · Resistive forces

Friction and drag oppose motion

Friction

Acts between two surfaces in contact, parallel to the surface, opposing the relative motion (or the tendency to move). No coefficients needed in 9702.

Viscous force / drag

Acts on an object moving through a fluid (liquid or gas). Air resistance is drag in air. Opposes motion.

The syllabus model

Drag increases as speed increases. That simple model is all you need.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Put your hand out of a car window. Going slowly, the air pushes your hand a little. Going fast, it pushes a lot. Air always pushes against the way you're going.

3.2.2 and 3.2.3 · Falling with air resistance

Why a skydiver stops speeding up

  1. Start: v = 0, so drag = 0. Resultant force = weight, so a = g.
  2. Speeding up: drag increases, resultant force (W − D) decreases, so acceleration decreases.
  3. Terminal velocity: drag = weight, resultant force = 0, a = 0, velocity constant.
  4. Parachute opens: drag suddenly much bigger than weight, resultant force upwards, so she decelerates. Drag falls as she slows.
  5. New, lower terminal velocity when drag again equals weight.

Trap

"The skydiver moves upwards when the parachute opens." No, she keeps falling, just more slowly. The acceleration is upwards, the velocity is still downwards.

3.3.1 and 3.3.2 · Conservation

Total momentum before = total momentum after

Principle of conservation of momentumThe total momentum of a system remains constant provided no resultant external force acts on the system.
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Worked example: collision

Trolley A (2.0 kg) at 3.0 m s⁻¹ hits stationary trolley B (1.0 kg). They stick together. Find their speed.

2.0 × 3.0 + 1.0 × 0 = (2.0 + 1.0)v → v = 2.0 m s⁻¹

Worked example: explosion (rifle recoil)

A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m s⁻¹. Total momentum before = 0.

0 = 0.010 × 400 + 4.0 × v → v = −1.0 m s⁻¹ (backwards)

Momentum is shared equally and oppositely, but kinetic energy is not: bullet 800 J, rifle 2 J. The energy came from the chemical energy of the propellant.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Two kids on ice skates push each other. The bigger kid rolls back slowly, the smaller one rolls back fast. The "push" each one gets is the same size, just in opposite directions.

3.3.3 and 3.3.4 · Types of collision

Momentum is always conserved. Kinetic energy may not be.

ElasticInelastic
Total momentumconservedconserved
Total kinetic energyconservednot conserved (some lost)
Total energyconservedconserved (KE → heat, sound, deformation)
Relative speedsspeed of approach = speed of separationseparation < approach

Worked example: elastic collision

A (2.0 kg) at 3.0 m s⁻¹ hits B (1.0 kg) at rest. The collision is elastic. Find both final velocities.

momentum: 6.0 = 2.0v_A + 1.0v_B relative speeds: 3.0 − 0 = v_B − v_A → v_B = v_A + 3.0 6.0 = 2.0v_A + v_A + 3.0 → v_A = 1.0 m s⁻¹, v_B = 4.0 m s⁻¹ check KE: before ½(2)(3²) = 9 J; after ½(2)(1²) + ½(1)(4²) = 1 + 8 = 9 J ✓

Compare the stick-together case on the previous slide: KE after = ½(3.0)(2.0²) = 6 J, so 3 J of kinetic energy was transferred to other forms.

Trap: "energy is lost in an inelastic collision"

Say kinetic energy is not conserved. Total energy is always conserved.

3.3.2 · Two dimensions

Conserve momentum in x and y separately

Worked example

x: 0.40 × 5.0 = 0.40 × 2.5 cos 60° + p_Bx → p_Bx = 2.0 − 0.50 = 1.50 N s y: 0 = 0.40 × 2.5 sin 60° + p_By → p_By = −0.866 N s |p_B| = √(1.50² + 0.866²) = 1.73 N s → v_B = 1.73/0.40 = 4.33 m s⁻¹ angle = tan⁻¹(0.866/1.50) = 30° below the original line

Check KE: before ½(0.40)(5.0²) = 5.0 J. After 1.25 J + 3.75 J = 5.0 J, so this one is elastic.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Before the crash, nothing was moving up or down the page. So after the crash, if one puck goes up, the other must go down by the same "amount of push".

Crash two trolleys

Choose masses and velocities (right is positive). Momentum is always conserved. Watch what happens to kinetic energy.

Exam traps · Topic 3

Where marks are lost

Patterns from 9702 examiner reports and mark schemes for dynamics. The Past papers slide shows the exact questions.

Incomplete law statements

Missing "resultant", writing "constant speed", or omitting "on different bodies" / "equal and opposite".

Learn the three laws word for word.

Wrong third-law pair

Naming weight and normal force as a pair.

Pairs act on different bodies and are the same type.

Rebound momentum

Δp = m(20 − 15) instead of m(20 + 15).

Velocity is a vector. Assign signs.

Using F = ma when mass changes

Jets, rockets, sand on a conveyor.

Use F = Δp/Δt = v × (Δm/Δt).

"KE conserved" in all collisions

Only elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy.

Momentum: always. KE: elastic only. Total energy: always.

Terminal velocity explanation

Saying "drag becomes greater than weight" while still speeding up, or "air resistance stops gravity".

Drag increases with speed until drag = weight, resultant = 0, a = 0.

Conservation condition

Stating the principle without "no resultant external force".

Always include the condition.

Weight vs mass

Writing weight in kg, or "weight is the gravity on an object".

W = mg in newtons. Weight is the effect of a gravitational field on a mass.
