Paper 2 · the big picture

Two hours, 60 marks, three sections

SectionWhat you doMarks
AOne compulsory data response question20
BOne essay from a choice of two, mainly microeconomics (this is where Topics 1–2 come up)20
COne essay from a choice of two, mainly macroeconomics20

Every essay has two parts: (a) worth 8 marks and (b) worth 12 marks. In past papers the choice is shown as Question 2 or Question 3, each with its own (a) and (b).

The three skills (AOs)

  • AO1 Knowledge and understanding: definitions, formulae, diagrams, examples.
  • AO2 Analysis: explaining why and how, step by step.
  • AO3 Evaluation: weighing up, then a judgement that answers the question.

On Papers 2 and 4, the syllabus weights them AO1 33%, AO2 37%, AO3 30%.

Suggested timing

About 40 minutes per 20-mark essay: roughly 15 minutes on (a) and 25 minutes on (b). Spend the first 2 minutes of each part decoding the question.

This timing is a suggestion, not a Cambridge rule.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

AO1 is saying what something is. AO2 is explaining how it works, like a chain of dominoes falling. AO3 is being the judge: you decide who wins, and you say why.

Part (a) · 8 marks · point-based

The 8-marker is marked 3 + 3 + 2

Part (a) is not marked in levels. Each correct point earns a mark, up to a cap for each skill. Examiner reports tell students to organise answers around this split.

SkillMaxWhat earns the marks (from real mark schemes)
AO13Accurate definition(s), the formula when asked, a correctly labelled diagram, accurate examples
AO23Explained analysis. Usually needs both things the question names (both goods, both directions, two determinants). Doing only one is often capped at 2.
AO321 for a considered evaluation (weighing up), 1 for a justified conclusion that answers the "consider" part

A structure that fits the marks

  1. Paragraph 1 (AO1): define every key term in the question precisely. Add the formula or diagram if asked, and an example.
  2. Paragraphs 2–3 (AO2): two developed analysis chains that answer the "explain" part, covering every case the question names.
  3. Paragraph 4 (AO3): answer the "consider" part. Say what it depends on, then finish with one sentence of judgement that uses the question's own words.

Trap: no conclusion, no AO3 mark

The second AO3 mark is reserved for a justified conclusion. In June 2023 (9708/22 Q2a) examiners reported that valid evaluation was "very rare", so most answers were capped at 4.

Part (b) · 12 marks · levels

The 12-marker: 8 for AO1 + AO2, 4 for AO3

Table A: AO1 + AO2 (8 marks)

LevelMarksDescriptor (mark scheme)
36–8Detailed knowledge and understanding of relevant concepts, using relevant explanations. In practice: balanced and accurate.
23–5Some relevant concepts, but explanations are limited, overgeneralised or contain inaccuracies. One-sided answers stop here.
11–2A small number of relevant points, limited by significant errors or omissions

Table B: AO3 (4 marks)

LevelMarksDescriptor (mark scheme)
23–4A justified conclusion or judgement that addresses the specific requirements of the question
11–2A vague or general conclusion or judgement
00No creditable response. A one-sided answer gets 0 here.

A structure that reaches Level 3 + Level 2

  1. Introduction: define the key terms and set up the two sides.
  2. Side 1: two developed chains, applied to the context in the question.
  3. Side 2: two developed chains for the alternative or the other view. Most questions name it: "or", "rather than", "than", "always".
  4. Evaluation: compare the two sides directly. Say which matters more, and when, and what it depends on.
  5. Conclusion: a clear answer to the exact question, with the main reason.

The maths of a one-sided answer

Maximum Level 2 for AO1/AO2 (5 marks) + 0 for AO3 = 5 out of 12, however good the writing is.

Key words 2 · analysis (AO1 + AO2)

Words that ask you to show how and why

WordOfficial meaningWhat to write
ExplainSet out purposes or reasons; make relationships clear; say why and/or how, supported with relevant evidenceThe most common (a) command word. Give a cause, then a mechanism, then an effect. Use "because", "this means", "therefore". Back it with an example, figure or diagram.
AnalyseExamine in detail to show meaning, identify elements and the relationship between themBreak the issue into parts and show how they connect, step by step. Multi-step chains, not lists.
DemonstrateShow how, or give an exampleUsually with a diagram or numbers. Show the change happening.

"With the help of…" is also an instruction

"With the help of a diagram(s)", "a formula" or "examples" means marks are reserved for it. In Nov 2024 9708/22 Q3(a), 2 of the 3 AO1 marks were for the PPC diagram itself. Drawing it badly or leaving it out throws those marks away.

Trap: asserting instead of explaining

Examiner reports repeatedly say points that are "asserted rather than explained" stay in Level 1–2. "A maximum price causes a shortage" is an assertion. "The price is set below equilibrium, so quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls, creating excess demand" is analysis.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Two friends both say their toy car is faster. Explaining how each car works is analysis. Racing them, saying which won and why, is evaluation.

Skill · AO2

Write chains, not lists

A developed point has at least three links: cause → mechanism → effect, ideally ending on the question's own words.

Asserted (Level 1)

"If demand is inelastic, the firm should raise its price."

Developed (Level 3)

"If PED is −0.4, a 10% price rise causes only a 4% fall in quantity demanded, because consumers have few close substitutes. Since the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price, total expenditure (P × Q) rises. So the firm's revenue increases."

Linking words that signal analysis

because · this means that · as a result · which leads to · therefore · so · ceteris paribus · in the short run… whereas in the long run…

Use numbers and diagrams as evidence

A short worked figure (e.g. PED −0.4) or a labelled diagram you refer to in the text ("as shown by the shift from S to S₁") makes a chain precise. Examiner reports say diagrams "used without explanation" earn little.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Don't just say "the cup fell". Say "I bumped the table, so the cup slid to the edge, so it fell". Every "so" is a link in your chain.

Skill · AO3

The evaluation toolkit for Topics 1–2

Most evaluation in these topics is an "it depends on…" argument followed by a judgement. Pick the factors that fit the question.

It depends on…

  • The size of the elasticity: PED, PES, YED, XED values
  • Time period: short run vs long run
  • The type of good: necessity, luxury, inferior, merit, public
  • The size of the change: a 1% vs a 20% price change
  • Accuracy of data: estimates use past data and may be out of date
  • Ceteris paribus: other factors rarely stay constant
  • Type of economy: market, mixed, planned; level of development
  • Who is affected: consumers, producers, government

A conclusion formula

Answer: "Overall, [X] is likely to be more [useful/significant] than [Y]…"

Reason: "…because [the strongest point from your analysis]…"

Condition: "…although this depends on [one factor], since [when the answer would change]."

This gives a direct answer, a justification and a condition, which is what Level 2 AO3 needs.

Trap: a summary is not evaluation

"In conclusion, PED and PES are both useful" repeats the essay and is vague: Level 1 at best. Examiner reports say summaries are unlikely to get more than Level 1 (1 mark).

Key terms · Topic 1 · syllabus 1.1–1.3

Scarcity, methodology and factors of production

TermDefinition to write in the exam
Scarcity (the economic problem)Wants are unlimited but the resources to satisfy them are finite, so choices must be made
Opportunity costThe next best alternative forgone when a choice is made
Three basic questionsWhat to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce
Economics as a social scienceIt studies human behaviour in society using a scientific method (models, assumptions, testing), but people do not behave like controlled lab variables
Positive statementA statement of fact that can be tested against evidence, e.g. "a tax raised the price by 10%"
Normative statementA value judgement that cannot be tested, often containing "should" or "ought"
Ceteris paribus"Other things being equal": all other factors are assumed to stay constant
Short run / long run / very long runShort run: at least one factor of production is fixed. Long run: all factors are variable. Very long run: technology and other factors outside the firm's control change.
Land · labour · capital · enterpriseNatural resources · human effort, physical and mental · man-made goods used to produce other goods · organising the other factors and taking risk
Rewards to factorsLand → rent · labour → wages · capital → interest · enterprise → profit
Human capital vs physical capitalHuman: the skills, knowledge and education of workers. Physical: man-made equipment such as machinery, buildings and infrastructure.
Division of labourSplitting production into separate tasks, each done by different workers
SpecialisationConcentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products (by people, firms, regions or countries)
EntrepreneurThe person who organises the other factors of production and bears the risk, in return for profit

Key terms · Topic 1 · syllabus 1.4–1.6

Economic systems, PPCs and types of goods

TermDefinition to write in the exam
Market economyResources are privately owned and allocated by the price mechanism (demand and supply), driven by the profit motive
Planned economyThe state owns resources and decides what, how and for whom to produce
Mixed economyBoth the private sector (via markets) and the public sector (via government) allocate resources
Production possibility curve (PPC)Shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, with given technology
Constant opportunity costA straight-line PPC: the same amount of one good is given up for each extra unit of the other, because resources are equally suited to both
Increasing opportunity costA PPC bowed out from the origin: more of one good must be given up for each extra unit of the other, because resources are not equally suited to both
Shift of the PPCOutward: more or better resources or technology (economic growth). Inward: resources lost, e.g. natural disaster or war.
Point inside / beyond the PPCInside: unemployed or inefficiently used resources. Beyond: unattainable with current resources and technology.
Free goodA good that is not scarce, so it has zero opportunity cost, e.g. air
Private good (economic good)Scarce, so it has an opportunity cost. Rival (one person's use reduces what is left) and excludable (non-payers can be stopped from using it).
Public goodNon-rival and non-excludable, e.g. street lighting, national defence. Leads to the free rider problem, so the market may not provide it.
Merit goodA good more beneficial than consumers realise, so with imperfect information it is under-consumed in a free market, e.g. education, vaccinations
Demerit goodA good more harmful than consumers realise, so with imperfect information it is over-consumed in a free market, e.g. cigarettes
Imperfect informationConsumers (or producers) lack full or accurate knowledge of a good's costs and benefits, so they make choices that are not in their best interest

Trap: wrong examples

Examiners marked roads, health care, parks and education as incorrect examples of public goods (Nov 2023 22 Q3a). Health care and education are merit goods. Being provided by the government does not make something a public good.

Key terms · Topic 2 · syllabus 2.1 and 2.4

Demand, supply and equilibrium

TermDefinition to write in the exam
Effective demandThe desire to buy a good backed by the ability (and willingness) to pay for it
Individual vs market demandIndividual: one consumer's demand. Market: the sum of all individual demands at each price. Supply works the same way.
Determinants of demandOwn price (movement), plus income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes and fashion, advertising, population, expectations, government policy (shifts)
Determinants of supplyOwn price (movement), plus costs of production, technology, indirect taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods the firm could produce, number of firms, weather, expectations (shifts)
Movement along vs shiftA movement (extension or contraction) is caused only by a change in the good's own price. A shift (increase or decrease) is caused by a change in any other determinant.
Market equilibriumThe price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price or quantity to change
DisequilibriumQuantity demanded does not equal quantity supplied: excess demand (shortage) or excess supply (surplus)
Joint demandGoods demanded together: complements, e.g. cars and petrol
Alternative (competitive) demandGoods that are substitutes, e.g. tea and coffee
Derived demandDemand for something because of the demand for what it helps produce, e.g. demand for steel from demand for cars
Joint supplyProducing one good automatically produces another, e.g. beef and leather
Functions of priceRationing: a higher price limits who buys a scarce good. Signalling: price changes transmit consumers' preferences to producers. Incentive: a higher price encourages firms to supply more.

Trap

When defining equilibrium, students forgot "no tendency to change" (Jun 2023 22 Q3a). Quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied is only half of it.

Key terms · Topic 2 · syllabus 2.2–2.3

The four elasticities, written to score

TermDefinitionFormula
PEDResponsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, ceteris paribus%ΔQd ÷ %ΔP
YEDResponsiveness of demand to a change in income, ceteris paribus%ΔQd ÷ %ΔY
XEDResponsiveness of demand for good X to a change in the price of good Y, ceteris paribus%ΔQd(X) ÷ %ΔP(Y)
PESResponsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's own price, ceteris paribus%ΔQs ÷ %ΔP

Values (PED and PES, ignoring sign)

  • 0: perfectly inelastic
  • between 0 and 1: inelastic (%ΔQ smaller than %ΔP)
  • 1: unitary elasticity
  • above 1: elastic (%ΔQ greater than %ΔP)
  • ∞: perfectly elastic

On a straight-line demand curve PED falls from elastic (top) through unitary (midpoint) to inelastic (bottom).

Signs

  • YED > 0 normal good: 0–1 a necessity, above 1 a luxury
  • YED < 0 inferior good
  • XED > 0 substitutes, XED < 0 complements, XED = 0 unrelated
  • The bigger the XED value, the closer the relationship

Factors affecting PED

Number and closeness of substitutes, share of income spent, necessity or luxury, habit or addiction, time period, how broadly the market is defined

Factors affecting PES

Spare capacity, stocks and ease of storage, mobility of factors of production, length of the production process, time period

PED and total expenditure

Inelastic: price ↑ → TE ↑, price ↓ → TE ↓. Elastic: price ↑ → TE ↓, price ↓ → TE ↑. Unitary: TE unchanged.

Trap: "a big change"

Mark schemes credit elastic or inelastic only when explained with proportionate (percentage) changes, "not large or small changes" (Nov 2023 22 Q2a). Examiners also saw XED formulae missing the % signs or written upside down (Jun 2023 21 Q3a).

Key terms · Topic 2 · syllabus 2.5

Consumer surplus and producer surplus

Consumer surplusThe difference between the maximum price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay. On a diagram: the area below the demand curve and above the price.
Producer surplusThe difference between the price producers actually receive and the minimum price they are willing to accept. On a diagram: the area above the supply curve and below the price.

How PED and PES decide the size of the change (2.5.4)

  • Supply falls (shifts left) and price rises: consumer surplus always falls. With inelastic demand, price rises a lot but quantity falls little; with elastic demand, quantity falls by more. The exact loss depends on how steep each curve is, so explain it with your diagram.
  • Demand rises (shifts right): producer surplus rises. With inelastic supply, price rises a lot and most of the gain goes to producers as a higher price; with elastic supply, output rises instead and price rises less.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

You would happily pay $5 for an ice cream, but it only costs $3. That extra $2 of happiness is your consumer surplus. The shop would sell it for $1 but gets $3: that $2 is its producer surplus.

Method · before you write

Decode every question in 4 steps

  1. Circle the command words. Most (a) parts have two: "explain… and consider…". The first is AO1 + AO2 (6 marks), the second is AO3 (2 marks).
  2. Underline the key terms. Every one needs a precise definition in your first paragraph.
  3. Box the trap words: always, or, the best, most, two, at the present time, in a growing economy. They tell you what the conclusion must answer.
  4. Plan in 60 seconds: the AO1 points, two analysis chains per side, and your one-sentence conclusion. Write the conclusion first in your plan so the whole essay heads towards it.
Example · Nov 2023 9708/22 Q2(a) · 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain what is meant by the term price elasticity of demand for a product and consider the extent to which it allows an entrepreneur to determine the effect of price changes on the total expenditure on their product.

"Price changes" means both rises and falls. The mark scheme gave a maximum of 1 mark to answers that only covered one direction.

Each worked example that follows has a writing pad. Plan or write your answer with your Pencil, then reveal the model answer to compare.

Past paper · Nov 2023 · 9708/22 Q2(a) · 8 marks

PED and total expenditure

QuestionWith the help of a formula, explain what is meant by the term price elasticity of demand for a product and consider the extent to which it allows an entrepreneur to determine the effect of price changes on the total expenditure on their product.

Decode: AO1 = meaning + formula + elastic vs inelastic using proportions. AO2 = what happens to TE when price rises and falls, for elastic and inelastic demand. AO3 = how far PED really lets the entrepreneur predict.

AO1Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded of a product to a change in its own price, ceteris paribus. PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP. Demand is price elastic when the percentage change in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage change in price (PED above 1, ignoring the sign), and price inelastic when it is smaller (PED between 0 and 1).

AO2Total expenditure is price × quantity. If demand is inelastic, for example PED = −0.4, a 10% price rise reduces quantity demanded by only 4%. The proportionate rise in price outweighs the fall in quantity, so total expenditure rises. A price cut would reduce total expenditure, because the small extra quantity sold does not make up for the lower price.

AO2If demand is elastic, for example PED = −2, a 10% price rise reduces quantity demanded by 20%, so total expenditure falls. A 10% price cut raises quantity by 20%, so total expenditure rises. If PED = −1, total expenditure stays the same.

AO3So PED tells the entrepreneur the direction of the change in total expenditure, but only to an extent. The estimate is based on past data, which may be inaccurate, and it may be expensive for a small business to calculate. PED also changes along a straight-line demand curve, so a value measured at one price may not hold after a large price change. Other factors, such as a rival's price cut or a change in incomes, can change total expenditure at the same time.

AO3Conclusion: PED allows an entrepreneur to predict the likely direction of the change in total expenditure for small price changes in the short run, but not its exact size, because the estimate may be out of date and other things rarely stay constant.

Why this scores 8: three AO1 points (meaning, formula, proportions), both directions for both types of demand, and an evaluation plus a conclusion that uses "the extent to which". Mark scheme reference: 9708/22 Nov 2023.

Past paper · Jun 2023 · 9708/22 Q2(a) · 8 marks

Consumer surplus and a cost rise

QuestionWith the help of a diagram(s), explain what is meant by consumer surplus and producer surplus and consider whether a rise in the price of a product because of higher costs of production is likely to always reduce the consumer surplus.

Decode: AO1 = both definitions + a labelled diagram. AO2 = supply shifts left, and show the CS change for elastic vs inelastic demand. AO3 = does CS always fall, and what decides by how much?

AO1Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay: the area below the demand curve and above the market price. Producer surplus is the difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they are willing to accept: the area above the supply curve and below the price. [Diagram: D and S crossing at P₁ and Q₁, with CS above P₁ and PS below P₁, as on the diagrams slide.]

AO2Higher costs of production shift the supply curve left from S to S₁. At the original price there is now excess demand, so price rises to P₂ and quantity falls to Q₂. Consumer surplus shrinks to the smaller triangle above P₂: consumers pay more for each unit and buy fewer units.

AO2The size of the fall depends on PED. With inelastic demand (a steep demand curve), the leftward shift in supply raises price a lot but cuts quantity only a little: consumers keep buying but pay much more. With elastic demand (a flatter curve), price rises less but quantity falls by more, because consumers switch to substitutes. Drawing both cases shows that the loss of consumer surplus differs in size and in how it happens.

AO3When higher costs raise the price, consumer surplus will always fall, because the price is higher and quantity is lower. What is not fixed is how much it falls. That depends on PED and on how much costs, and so supply, change.

AO3Conclusion: a cost-driven price rise is likely to always reduce consumer surplus, but the extent of the reduction depends on the price elasticity of demand.

The mark scheme accepted "will always fall" as the conclusion, with the extent depending on PED. Examiner report: evaluation was "very rare", so most answers stopped at 4.

Past paper · Nov 2024 · 9708/22 Q3(a) · 8 marks

Constant vs increasing opportunity cost

QuestionWith the help of a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram(s), explain the difference between constant and increasing opportunity costs and consider how choices in deciding which type of goods to produce in the short run may influence future economic growth.

Decode: AO1 = PPC diagram(s) with a straight line and a curve, product-labelled axes, touching both axes, plus the explanation. AO2 = consumer goods vs capital goods now, and the effect on future growth. AO3 = a judgement on the trade-off.

AO1A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed. [Diagram: capital goods on the vertical axis, consumer goods on the horizontal axis. A straight-line PPC and a PPC bowed out from the origin, both touching both axes.] A straight-line PPC shows constant opportunity cost: the same amount of capital goods is given up for each extra unit of consumer goods, because resources are equally suited to both. A PPC bowed out from the origin shows increasing opportunity cost: more and more capital goods must be given up for each extra unit of consumer goods, because resources are not equally suited to producing both.

AO2If the economy chooses a point with more consumer goods now, living standards rise in the short run, but fewer capital goods are produced. With less investment in machinery and infrastructure, productive capacity grows slowly, so the PPC shifts outwards only a little in the future.

AO2If it chooses more capital goods now, consumption is lower in the short run, giving an opportunity cost in living standards. However, the extra capital raises productive capacity, so the PPC shifts further outwards, allowing more of both goods in the future.

AO3The effect depends on whether the capital goods are used productively and on how long the economy can accept lower consumption. A very poor economy may not be able to cut consumption further. Conclusion: choosing more capital goods in the short run is likely to give faster and more sustainable future growth, as long as the investment raises productivity and people can afford the short-run sacrifice.

Mark scheme: 1 mark each for the curved line, the straight line and the explanation. Analysis covers consumer goods (short-run benefit, may not be sustainable) vs capital goods (long-run growth).

Past paper · Nov 2023 · 9708/22 Q3(a) · 8 marks

Free goods vs public goods

QuestionWith the help of examples, explain the difference between free goods and public goods and consider the view that public goods can never be provided in a market economy.

Decode: AO1 = define both, with a correct example of each. AO2 = link non-excludability and non-rivalry to the free rider problem, so no price can be charged. AO3 = "never": consider government funding and quasi-public goods.

AO1A free good is not scarce, so it has zero opportunity cost and needs no factors of production, e.g. fresh air. A public good is scarce and costs resources to produce, but it is non-excludable (people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting) and non-rival (one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others), e.g. street lighting.

AO2Because a public good is non-excludable, people can benefit without paying, so they have an incentive to be free riders. Because it is non-rival, an extra user costs nothing, so there is no reason to exclude anyone even if it were possible.

AO2As a result, firms cannot collect enough revenue to make a profit. In a market economy, where resources are allocated by the price mechanism and the profit motive, the good is unlikely to be supplied at all. This is a market failure, and it is why governments usually provide public goods, paid for by taxation.

AO3For pure public goods, the view is largely correct: a market economy will not provide them unless the government pays for them. However, some quasi-public goods, such as a toll road, can be made excludable with technology, so a price can be charged and private firms can supply them.

AO3Conclusion: pure public goods are very unlikely ever to be provided by a market economy, but "never" is too strong for quasi-public goods, which the market can supply when exclusion is possible.

Examiner report: students confused non-excludable with non-rival, gave roads, health care, parks or education as public goods, and asserted the free rider problem without linking it to the two characteristics.

Past paper · Nov 2024 · 9708/22 Q2(b) · 12 marks

YED or PED during economic growth?

QuestionAssess whether knowledge of income elasticity of demand or price elasticity of demand for its product is more useful to a business aiming to increase the total expenditure on its product during a period of economic growth.

Decode: Two concepts, so both must be analysed and their usefulness compared, not just defined. The context "economic growth" (rising incomes) must run through every point. The mark scheme asked for focus on how each measure is used, related to growth.

AO1Income elasticity of demand (YED) measures the responsiveness of demand to a change in income, ceteris paribus: %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY. Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the product's own price: %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP. Total expenditure is price × quantity. During economic growth, real incomes are rising.

AO2The case for YED. In a growing economy, incomes rise, so YED tells the business directly how demand will shift. If its product is a normal good with YED of, say, +2 (a luxury), a 5% rise in incomes increases demand by 10%, so demand shifts right and total expenditure rises without any change in price. If YED is negative (an inferior good), demand falls as incomes rise, warning the business to change its product range towards normal goods. Because growth is the main change in the market, YED is the measure most closely linked to it.

AO2The case for PED. YED says nothing about pricing. PED tells the business whether to raise or cut price to increase total expenditure: with inelastic demand, a price rise increases total expenditure; with elastic demand, a price cut does. During growth, rising incomes may make consumers less sensitive to price, making demand more inelastic, so the firm could raise price and increase total expenditure further.

AO2Limitations of both. Both are estimates from past data, which may be inaccurate. Their values change as incomes rise, so a value measured before the growth period may now be wrong. Ceteris paribus rarely holds: rivals may change prices during growth.

AO3Evaluation. The two measures answer different questions. YED tells the business what will happen to demand because of the growth itself, while PED tells it how to set price to take advantage of that. In a period of economic growth the main driver of total expenditure is rising income, so YED is the more relevant starting point, especially for luxury products with high YED. For a necessity with a low YED, demand barely rises with income, so pricing decisions based on PED matter more.

AO3Conclusion: during economic growth, YED is likely to be more useful because it directly predicts the shift in demand caused by rising incomes. PED becomes more useful when the product's YED is low, and both are only as reliable as the data they are based on.

Mark scheme: both concepts, applied to growth, with a justified conclusion. A one-sided response earns no evaluation marks.

Past paper · Jun 2024 · 9708/23 Q2(b) · 12 marks

Electric cars: XED or YED?

Context: From 2030, the only new car production allowed in the United Kingdom (UK) will be for electric cars.Assess whether cross elasticity of demand is likely to be more important in determining the demand for electric cars than income elasticity of demand.

Decode: Apply everything to electric cars. XED covers substitutes (petrol cars, public transport) and complements (electricity, charging points). YED covers cars as an expensive, income-sensitive purchase. Include the weaknesses of both, then judge "more important".

AO1Cross elasticity of demand (XED) is the responsiveness of demand for one good to a change in the price of another good, %ΔQd(X) ÷ %ΔP(Y). It is positive for substitutes and negative for complements. Income elasticity of demand (YED) is the responsiveness of demand to a change in income, %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY.

AO2The case for XED. Petrol cars are close substitutes for electric cars, so XED between them is likely to be positive and high. If petrol prices rise, petrol cars become more expensive to run, and buyers switch to electric cars, shifting demand for electric cars to the right. Charging points and electricity are complements: if electricity prices rise, or charging points are scarce, the effective cost of running an electric car rises and demand falls. XED is negative here.

AO2The case for YED. Electric cars are expensive and take a large share of income, so they are likely to be a normal good, probably with YED above 1. When incomes rise, demand rises more than proportionately; in a recession, buyers delay purchases. Whatever the price of substitutes, many households cannot afford an electric car unless incomes are high enough.

AO2Weaknesses. Both values are estimates and may be out of date. Once new petrol cars cannot be produced, the substitute relationship weakens, so XED with new petrol cars becomes less relevant over time. Attitudes to the environment and government subsidies also affect demand, and neither elasticity captures them.

AO3Evaluation. At present, while petrol cars are still widely available, XED is likely to be more important, because the running-cost comparison with petrol cars and the availability of charging points drive the decision to switch. After 2030, new petrol cars will no longer be produced, so the choice is mainly whether to buy a car at all, and when. That decision depends more on income, so YED becomes more important.

AO3Conclusion: XED is likely to be more important now, but YED is likely to become more important after 2030, once electric cars are the only new cars available. The answer depends on the time period.

Mark scheme: XED with substitutes and complements, YED and its relevance, weaknesses of both, and a justified comparison. A one-sided response cannot gain evaluation marks.

Past paper · Nov 2024 · 9708/21 Q2(b) · 12 marks

Is farm supply more or less price elastic?

QuestionAssess whether the supply of agricultural products is likely to be more price elastic or less price elastic than the supply of manufactured products.

Decode: Go factor by factor through what determines PES, comparing the two types of product every time. Conclude with "more" or "less", with conditions.

AO1Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the product's own price, ceteris paribus: %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. Supply is elastic if quantity supplied changes by a greater percentage than price, and inelastic if by a smaller percentage.

AO2Why agricultural supply is likely to be less elastic. The production process is long: a rice crop takes months to grow, so after a price rise farmers cannot increase output until the next harvest, and supply is very inelastic in the short run. Many farm products are perishable and hard to store, so farmers cannot hold stocks to release when prices rise. Land is fixed in supply and not very mobile between uses, and supply depends on the weather, which farmers cannot control.

AO2Why manufactured supply is likely to be more elastic. Factories can often raise output quickly by adding shifts or overtime if there is spare capacity. Most manufactured goods can be stored, so firms keep stocks and can supply more immediately. Labour and machinery can be moved between product lines more easily.

AO2The other side. Not all agricultural supply is inelastic: products that can be stored, such as grain in silos, or crops with short growing cycles, can respond faster. Manufacturers working at full capacity, or making complex products with long lead times such as aircraft, have inelastic supply in the short run. In the long run, both become more elastic as new land is farmed or new factories are built.

AO3Evaluation and conclusion: agricultural supply is likely to be less price elastic than manufactured supply, mainly because of the long, weather-dependent production period and the difficulty of storage. This is most true in the short run and for perishable crops. It is less true for storable produce, or when manufacturers have no spare capacity. Over the long run the gap narrows.

Mark scheme: the expected conclusion was that agricultural supply is likely to be less elastic, though any valid, justified response was accepted. Factors listed: number of producers, spare capacity, storage, time period, factor mobility, length of production.

Past paper · Nov 2024 · 9708/22 Q3(b) · 12 marks

Is a market economy always the best?

QuestionAssess whether a market economy is always the best economic system to effectively answer the three basic questions of resource allocation.

Decode: Use the three questions (what, how, for whom) as your structure. "Always the best" requires a comparison with at least one other system, planned or mixed. The mark scheme said this.

AO1Every economy must decide what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce because resources are scarce. In a market economy, these questions are answered by the price mechanism: private individuals and firms, driven by self-interest and the profit motive, respond to the prices set by demand and supply.

AO2Strengths of the market economy. What: consumer sovereignty, because rising demand raises price, which signals to firms and gives them an incentive to produce more of what consumers want. How: competition pushes firms to use the cheapest methods and to innovate, so resources are used efficiently. For whom: goods go to those willing and able to pay, so price rations scarce goods without the need for a large bureaucracy.

AO2Weaknesses. What: public goods such as street lighting are not provided because of the free rider problem; merit goods such as education are under-consumed and demerit goods over-consumed because of imperfect information. For whom: goods go to those with income, so the poor may go without essentials, and inequality can grow.

AO2The alternatives. A planned economy can provide public and merit goods and share output more equally, but without price signals and the profit motive it often produces the wrong goods inefficiently. A mixed economy uses markets for most private goods and government for public goods, merit goods and redistribution, aiming to keep the strengths of both.

AO3Evaluation and conclusion: the market economy answers "what" and "how" well for ordinary private goods, but it is not always the best, because it fails to answer "for whom" fairly and fails to provide public and merit goods. A mixed economy is likely to answer all three questions more effectively, although how well it works depends on how efficient the government is and how much it intervenes.

Mark scheme: advantages and disadvantages of the market system for all three questions, compared with at least one other system. A one-sided response earns no evaluation marks.

Exam traps · from examiner reports

Where marks are lost on Topics 1–2 essays

Patterns from 9708 Paper 2 examiner reports (June 2023, November 2023) and mark schemes.

Describing instead of assessing

Defining PED and XED at length when asked how useful they are to a business.

Spend one paragraph on definitions, then write about use and usefulness.

One-sided (b) answers

Only one concept or side analysed.

Max Level 2 and 0 for AO3. Always analyse the alternative.

Summary as "evaluation"

Repeating points in the conclusion.

Compare, rank and give a judgement with a condition.

Ignoring the context

General answers with no link to cars, smartphones or a growing economy.

Name the context in every paragraph. Mark schemes cap answers that ignore it.

Vague elasticity

"A big change in demand".

Use percentage (proportionate) changes and numbers.

Wrong public good examples

Roads, health care, education, parks.

Use street lighting, national defence, lighthouses.

Diagram errors

Movement drawn instead of a shift, AD/AS for one market, PPC not touching the axes, unlabelled axes.

Label everything and refer to the diagram in your text.

Answering a different question

"Most significant" instead of "most immediate"; classification vs provision.

Box the trap words before you plan.
