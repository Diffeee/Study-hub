1.1 · Physical quantities

A quantity is a number times a unit

A physical quantity is anything you can measure. It always has two parts: a numerical magnitude and a unit. "Length = 3.2" is meaningless. "Length = 3.2 m" is a physical quantity.

quantity = number × unit    e.g. 3.2 m = 3.2 × (1 m)

Because a quantity is a product, you can do algebra on units. This is why a table heading is written length / m: dividing the quantity by its unit leaves a pure number that goes in the table.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

If I say "I'll give you 5", you would ask "5 what? Sweets? Biscuits? Elephants?" The number alone tells you nothing. The unit is the "what". Physics always needs both.

Table headings in Paper 3Write quantity / unit, for example I / mA. The syllabus also accepts "I (mA)" or "I in mA", but never "I mA" or just "mA".

At A Level (topic 15) you add amount of substance, mole (mol). Every other unit in the syllabus is derived from these.

Trap: these are not base units

newton, joule, coulomb, volt, °C, gram. A coulomb is A s. The base unit of mass is the kilogram, even though it has a prefix.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Base units are like LEGO bricks. You can't break them into smaller bricks. Every other unit is a model built by sticking these bricks together.

1.2.3 · Homogeneity

Both sides must have the same base units

An equation is homogeneous if every term, on both sides, has the same SI base units. You can add or equate only like with like.

Worked example: is s = ut + ½at² homogeneous?

s → m ut → m s⁻¹ × s = m ½at² → m s⁻² × s² = m (½ has no unit)

Every term is in metres, so the equation is homogeneous.

Worked example: find the unit of a constant

Drag force F = kv². Rearrange: k = F/v² → kg m s⁻² ÷ m² s⁻² = kg m⁻¹.

Trap: homogeneous does not mean correct

s = ut + at² is homogeneous but wrong. Base units cannot check pure numbers like ½ or 2π, and cannot spot a missing term. Homogeneity is necessary, not sufficient.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

You can't say "3 apples = 3 bananas". Even though the numbers match, the things don't. An equation must match apples with apples.

Trap: squared and cubed units

The prefix is cubed or squared too. 1 cm = 10⁻² m, so 1 cm² = (10⁻²)² m² = 10⁻⁴ m² and 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³. 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², 1 mm³ = 10⁻⁹ m³.

Density: 1 g cm⁻³ = 10⁻³ kg ÷ 10⁻⁶ m³ = 1000 kg m⁻³.

Capital matters: m (milli) and M (mega) differ by 10⁹. Write μ carefully, not u.

1.3.1 · Errors

Systematic errors and random errors

Systematic error

  • Every reading is wrong by the same amount or the same fraction, in the same direction.
  • Causes: a zero error (meter reads 0.02 A with no current), a badly calibrated instrument, reading a scale from the same wrong angle every time.
  • Repeating and averaging does not remove it.
  • Fix: check and correct the zero, recalibrate, or use a graph intercept.

Random error

  • Readings are scattered unpredictably above and below the true value.
  • Causes: reaction time with a stop-watch, a fluctuating reading, judging when a pendulum passes a point.
  • Fix: repeat and average, or plot a graph and draw a best-fit line.

Zero error example

A micrometer reads +0.03 mm when fully closed. A wire reads 0.52 mm. True diameter = 0.52 − 0.03 = 0.49 mm. Every reading carries that same +0.03 mm, which is why it is systematic.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

A bathroom scale that always adds 2 kg is a systematic error. It is wrong the same way every time. A wobbly scale that shows a different number each time you step on it has random error.

1.3.2 · Precision and accuracy

Close together versus close to the truth

AccuracyHow close a measurement (or the mean of measurements) is to the true value. Reduced by systematic error.
PrecisionHow close repeated measurements are to each other (small spread). Reduced by random error.
Explain it to a 5-year-old

Throwing balls at a bin. If all your balls land in one tight pile next to the bin, you are precise but not accurate. If they land in the bin, you are accurate.

1.3.3 · Combining uncertainties

Absolute, fractional and percentage uncertainty

If x = 4.0 ± 0.2 cm, the absolute uncertainty is Δx = 0.2 cm, the fractional uncertainty is Δx/x = 0.05, and the percentage uncertainty is 5%.

Worked example

I = 2.0 ± 0.1 A and R = 10.0 ± 0.2 Ω. Find P = I²R with its uncertainty.

%I = 0.1/2.0 × 100 = 5% %R = 0.2/10.0 × 100 = 2% %P = 2 × 5 + 2 = 12% P = 2.0² × 10.0 = 40 W ΔP = 12% of 40 = 4.8 W P = 40 ± 5 W

Give the absolute uncertainty to 1 (sometimes 2) significant figures, and round the value to the same decimal place. For repeated readings in the practical, uncertainty = half the range.

1.3.3 · Uncertainty in practice

Three situations that catch students

1. Radius from diameter

d = 4.00 ± 0.02 mm (0.5%). Then r = 2.00 ± 0.01 mm. The percentage stays 0.5%. Halving the value halves the absolute uncertainty too.

2. Taking a difference

x₁ = 50.0 ± 0.5 cm, x₂ = 48.0 ± 0.5 cm. x₁ − x₂ = 2.0 ± 1.0 cm, a 50% uncertainty. Subtracting close values makes the % uncertainty huge.

3. Area of a wire

A = πd²/4. If %d = 2%, then %A = 4%. π and 4 are exact.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

If you are not sure about a number, and you multiply it by itself, you get twice as unsure. Mistakes pile up; they never cancel in these rules.

1.4.1 · Scalars and vectors

Size only, or size and direction

ScalarA quantity with magnitude only.
VectorA quantity with magnitude and direction.
ScalarsVectors
distance, speed, mass, time, energy, work, power, pressure, density, temperature, volume, chargedisplacement, velocity, acceleration, force (including weight), momentum, electric field strength

Trap: pressure and work are scalars

Work is made from two vectors (F and s) but is a scalar. Pressure acts in all directions in a fluid, so it is a scalar. A common Paper 1 question asks for the pair "one scalar, one vector".

Explain it to a 5-year-old

"Walk 10 steps" is a scalar. "Walk 10 steps towards the door" is a vector. The direction is the extra bit.

1.4.2 · Adding and subtracting

Tip to tail, then measure the gap

  • Perpendicular: Pythagoras for size, tan⁻¹ for angle. Always give the direction.
  • Not perpendicular: scale drawing, or the cosine rule a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A (listed in the syllabus maths requirements).
  • Subtracting: A − B = A + (−B). Reverse B, then add tip to tail.

Worked example: change in velocity

A ball hits a wall at 5.0 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 4.0 m s⁻¹. Take towards the wall as positive. Δv = v − u = (−4.0) − (+5.0) = −9.0 m s⁻¹, so 9.0 m s⁻¹ away from the wall. Not 1.0 m s⁻¹.

A car turns from 10 m s⁻¹ north to 10 m s⁻¹ east. Its speed is unchanged, but Δv = 10 east + 10 south = 14.1 m s⁻¹ towards the south-east.

1.4.3 · Resolving

Split one vector into two perpendicular parts

Worked example

A 50 N force at 30° above the horizontal. Horizontal = 50 cos 30° = 43.3 N. Vertical = 50 sin 30° = 25.0 N. Check: √(43.3² + 25²) = 50 N.

On a slope at angle θ

Weight mg splits into mg sin θ down the slope and mg cos θ into the slope. Check with θ = 0: flat ground gives 0 along the slope, which makes sense.

Explain it to a 5-year-old

Pulling a toy cart with a rope held up high: part of your pull drags the cart forward, and part lifts it up. Resolving just finds out how much goes each way.

Exam traps · Topic 1

Where marks are lost

Patterns from 9702 examiner reports and mark schemes for this topic. The Past papers slide shows the exact questions.

Wrong "base units"

Answering N, J or C when asked for SI base units, or listing °C.

Reduce everything to kg, m, s, A, K.

Prefix and power-of-ten slips

Converting cm² or mm³ as if they were cm or mm. Not checking whether an answer is a sensible size.

Square or cube the prefix. Sanity-check every answer.

"Averaging removes systematic error"

It does not. Averaging reduces only random error.

Systematic: recalibrate or correct zero error.

Precision and accuracy swapped

Saying a precise instrument is accurate.

Precision = spread. Accuracy = closeness to true value.

Uncertainty with powers

Forgetting to double the % for a squared term, or doubling it when finding radius from diameter.

Power n multiplies the %. Halving does not change the %.

Too many significant figures

Writing 40.0 ± 4.8 W.

Uncertainty to 1 s.f., value to match: 40 ± 5 W.

sin and cos swapped

The most common resolving error.

Draw the triangle. Adjacent to θ gets cos.

Adding vectors as numbers

3 N + 4 N at right angles is 5 N, not 7 N. A rebound Δv is a sum of speeds, not a difference.

Choose a positive direction first.
